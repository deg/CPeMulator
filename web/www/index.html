    Getting started with CP/M

    CP/M is the operating system most 8-bit business computers ran around 1980, before MS-DOS (which borrowed much of it). A> means it's ready for a command on drive A:. Type a command and press Enter; upper or lower case are the same.

    • Built in: DIR lists files, TYPE README.TXT shows a text file, ERA OLD.TXT erases one, REN NEW.TXT=OLD.TXT renames one. * and ? are wildcards: DIR *.COM.
    • Programs are the .COM files: type the name without .COM. STAT shows free space, PIP COPY.TXT=README.TXT copies a file, DDT is the debugger.
    • The editor needs a file name: ED HELLO.TXT. (Without one, ED just says DISK OR DIRECTORY FULL, a quirk of the original.) At its * prompt, I starts inserting text, ^Z (Ctrl, then Z) stops, B#T shows the whole file, and E saves and quits.
    • A first program: ASM DUMP assembles Digital Research's sample source, LOAD DUMP turns it into DUMP.COM, and DUMP DUMP.COM runs it on itself.
    • Keys: ^C at the prompt restarts CP/M (a "warm boot"); ^S pauses scrolling output and any key resumes it; Backspace erases. Some keys are kept by your browser (Ctrl-W, Ctrl-T, Ctrl-N on Windows and Linux): click the Ctrl-W, Ctrl-T or Ctrl-N button under the screen (or Ctrl + …, then any letter).
    Adding software, and keeping your work
    • Get software (top right) has the desktop version's software: Zork, Turbo Pascal, WordStar, MBASIC, dBASE II, compilers and games. To get it all:
      1. Click Get software, then Get all (or Get on just the programs you want). Your browser downloads them. It may ask once whether this site may download several files: choose Allow, since blocked downloads are dropped without a word. Nothing is on your disk yet.
      2. Open your Downloads folder, select all the new .zip files, and drop them on this page together (or click the drop box in Get software and choose them). This is the step that puts them on your disk.
      The page puts the right files from each archive on A: (leaving out scanned manuals and the like, as the desktop version does) and installs WordStar for this screen. Each program then shows On disk, and its name opens a note on how to use it. If a site can't be reached, the program's row says Didn't download; if a file just never arrives, use get again, or the program's original site, listed when you open its row. The archives aren't hosted here: they come from the sites that preserve them, so mind each program's license. For more software, see where to find CP/M software.
    • Your own files: Add files (in Your disk), or drag files onto this page, to put them on A:. A .zip is unpacked onto the disk, old DOS-era archives included. Adding files restarts CP/M; if a program is running, the page asks first.
    • Save disk (in Your disk, or the link in the status line) saves drive A: as a .dsk file: that file is your real copy. The disk is also kept in this browser between visits, but browsers clear their storage on their own (Safari after a week without a visit; any browser when space runs low or history is cleared). The status line says when the disk has changed since your last saved copy. Load disk brings a saved .dsk back.
    • Your disk also lists the files on A: (click one to save a copy of it) and has Fresh disk to start over.
    Run it on your own computer

    This page is the same emulator, compiled to WebAssembly. The desktop version runs in a terminal window and adds:

    • A debug monitor: press Ctrl-\ at any moment for breakpoints, single-stepping, disassembly, and the Z80's registers and memory.
    • Real files: disks are .dsk files on your computer, written back when you quit, with nothing a browser can clear. Disks saved from this page work there.
    • More drives: up to 16, A: to P:, and host files loaded straight onto a disk from the command line.
    • A ready-made library: make setup downloads classic software (Zork, Turbo Pascal, WordStar set up for your terminal, MBASIC, dBASE II, games) and make play boots with all of it.
    • Your own keyboard: no keys taken by a browser, and --arrows makes the arrow keys move the cursor in WordStar-style editors.
    • More speed and control: native code, an exact clock (--mhz=2 for fast action games), or as fast as your machine goes.

    It needs a Rust toolchain; the quick start goes from download to a running CP/M in four steps.

    About CPeMulator

    CPeMulator emulates a Zilog Z80 processor and a small machine around it, then boots the genuine Digital Research CP/M 2.2, the same code that ran on real hardware, rather than imitating it. The fresh disk holds DRI's own utilities, which DRI's successor allows anyone to redistribute. Everything runs in your browser; nothing you type or upload is sent anywhere.
